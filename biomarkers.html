Metabolic & Glucose

▲
Fasting Glucose i
Optimal: 4.0 to 5.0 mmol/L
mmol/L
,
Measures blood sugar after an overnight fast. High readings signal poor glucose control and rising diabetes risk, often years before diagnosis. Low readings (below range) can indicate poor meal timing or overtraining without adequate fuel.
HbA1c i
Optimal: <5.4% (<36 mmol/mol)
Lee: 33 mmol/mol ≈ 5.2% · NHS 25 Jun 2024
%
,
A 3-month average of blood sugar control, the single best marker for metabolic ageing. High HbA1c accelerates ageing across nearly every organ system via glycation damage. Low is rarely a concern outside of anaemia distorting the result.
Fasting Insulin i
Optimal: <8 mIU/L
mIU/L
,
Fasting insulin reveals problems years before glucose does. High insulin means your body is working overtime to manage sugar, an early warning of insulin resistance. Low insulin alongside normal glucose is the sign of a metabolically efficient body.
HOMA-IR i
Optimal: <1.0 (insulin resistance proxy)
ratio
,
A calculated ratio of glucose and insulin, this is the earliest reliable insulin resistance marker available. High HOMA-IR predicts future diabetes, fatty liver, and cardiovascular disease. Low is the goal, it means your cells respond efficiently to insulin.

Cardiovascular
,
▲
Total Cholesterol i
Optimal: <5.0 mmol/L
mmol/L
,
Total cholesterol alone is a blunt tool, but extremes matter. Very high levels warrant closer inspection of LDL and particle count. Very low levels can affect hormone production, since cholesterol is the raw material for testosterone and other hormones.
LDL Cholesterol i
Optimal: <2.5 mmol/L
mmol/L
,
Often called bad cholesterol, LDL carries fat through the bloodstream and, at high levels, drives arterial plaque formation. High LDL is a major cardiovascular risk factor. Low LDL is generally protective, though extremely low levels rarely cause issues.
HDL Cholesterol i
Optimal: >1.5 mmol/L
mmol/L
,
Good cholesterol, HDL clears excess cholesterol from arteries and transports it back to the liver. High HDL is protective and associated with longevity. Low HDL is an independent cardiovascular risk factor, even if other markers look fine.
Triglycerides i
Optimal: <1.0 mmol/L
mmol/L
,
Triglycerides are stored fat circulating in the blood, heavily influenced by sugar and alcohol intake. High triglycerides signal poor metabolic health and raise heart disease risk. Low is favourable and reflects good dietary control.
hsCRP (High-Sensitivity) i
Optimal: <0.5 mg/L
mg/L
,
A sensitive marker of systemic inflammation, the single biggest driver of accelerated ageing. High hsCRP is linked to heart disease, cognitive decline, and nearly every age-related condition. Low is the target, it means your body isn't fighting chronic low-grade inflammation.
Systolic Blood Pressure i
Optimal: 100 to 120 mmHg
Lee: 120 mmHg · current confirmed value
mmHg
,
The pressure in your arteries when your heart beats. High blood pressure silently damages blood vessels, the heart, kidneys, and brain over years. Low blood pressure is usually fine unless it causes dizziness or fatigue.
Resting Heart Rate i
Optimal: <55 bpm
Lee: 47 bpm
bpm
,
Your heart rate at complete rest, a strong proxy for cardiovascular fitness. High resting heart rate suggests poor conditioning or stress. Low resting heart rate (within reason) reflects a strong, efficient heart, elite athletes often sit in the 40s.

Hormonal
,
▲
Total Testosterone i
Optimal male: 20 to 35 nmol/L
Lee: 19.4 nmol/L · NHS 13 Aug 2026
nmol/L
,
The primary male hormone, driving muscle mass, bone density, mood, libido, and motivation. Low testosterone accelerates muscle loss, fat gain, and cognitive decline with age. High levels (within physiological range) support vitality, but should always be medically supervised.
Free Testosterone i
Optimal male: >0.3 nmol/L
Lee: 0.443 nmol/L (443 pmol/L) · NHS 10 Mar 2026
nmol/L
,
The active, usable portion of your total testosterone, not bound to proteins. This matters more than total testosterone for how you actually feel. Low free testosterone can cause fatigue and low libido even when total testosterone looks normal.
IGF-1 (Growth Hormone proxy) i
Optimal: 150 to 280 ng/mL
ng/mL
,
A proxy for growth hormone activity, driving tissue repair, muscle maintenance, and recovery. Low IGF-1 is linked to reduced muscle mass and slower recovery with age. Very high levels need medical review, since IGF-1 has complex effects on cancer risk.
DHEA-S i
Optimal 60+: >150 µg/dL
µg/dL
,
A precursor hormone your body uses to produce testosterone and other hormones, and a marker of adrenal reserve. Low DHEA-S is common with age and linked to reduced vitality and stress resilience. High is generally favourable in older adults.
Cortisol (morning) i
Optimal: 300 to 500 nmol/L
nmol/L
,
Your primary stress hormone, essential in the right amount, damaging in excess. High morning cortisol, especially if chronic, breaks down muscle and worsens insulin resistance. Low cortisol can cause fatigue and poor stress tolerance.
TSH (Thyroid) i
Optimal: 1.0 to 2.0 mIU/L
mIU/L
,
Thyroid stimulating hormone, the control signal for your metabolism. High TSH suggests an underactive thyroid, associated with fatigue, weight gain, and slowed metabolism. Low TSH suggests an overactive thyroid, which carries its own risks.

Body Composition

Body Fat Percentage (DEXA) i
Optimal 60+: <20% male / <28% female
Lee: 9.6%
%
,
The percentage of your body that is fat tissue, measured accurately only by DEXA. High body fat, particularly visceral fat, drives inflammation and metabolic disease. Low body fat within a healthy range is protective, but extremely low levels can affect hormone production.
Lean Muscle Mass (DEXA) i
Optimal: preserve / increase vs. age norm
Lee: 75 kg
kg
,
Muscle, organs, and bone, everything that isn't fat. This is your metabolic engine and your best defence against frailty in later life. Low or declining lean mass predicts loss of independence with age. High and stable lean mass is one of the strongest longevity markers there is.
Bone Density, T-score (DEXA) i
Optimal: >+1.0 SD vs. young-adult mean
Lee: 3.6
T-score
,
Measured by DEXA, your bone mineral density compared to a healthy young adult of the same sex, expressed as a T-score in standard deviations. Higher (more positive) is better, it means stronger, denser bone and lower fracture risk. Below -1.0 indicates osteopenia, below -2.5 indicates osteoporosis, both accelerate functional ageing.
Visceral Fat Area i
Optimal: <100 cm² (DEXA)
Lee: 54.6 cm²
cm²
,
Fat stored around your internal organs, far more dangerous than fat under the skin. High visceral fat is strongly linked to heart disease, diabetes, and inflammation, even in people who look lean. Low visceral fat is one of the best things you can optimise for healthspan.
BMI i
Optimal longevity: 22 to 25
kg/m²
,
A simple weight-to-height ratio, useful as a rough screen but blind to muscle versus fat. High BMI in a muscular person can be misleading, always read alongside body fat %. Low BMI in older adults can indicate insufficient muscle mass, which is a genuine risk factor.

Functional Fitness

VO₂ Max i
Excellent 60+: >40 mL/kg/min
Lee: 50 mL/kg/min
mL/kg/min
,
The maximum amount of oxygen your body can use during exercise, widely regarded as the single strongest predictor of longevity of any biomarker. High VO2 Max means your heart, lungs, and muscles work together efficiently. Low VO2 Max is one of the most modifiable risk factors for early death.
Grip Strength (dominant hand) i
Optimal male 60+: >40 kg
kg
,
A simple squeeze test that predicts overall strength, frailty risk, and even mortality in older adults. High grip strength correlates with better outcomes across nearly every health measure. Low grip strength is an early, often-missed warning sign of declining functional capacity.
HRV (Heart Rate Variability) i
Optimal 60+: >40 ms (RMSSD)
ms
,
Heart rate variability, the tiny variations between heartbeats, reflects how well your nervous system recovers from stress. High HRV indicates good recovery capacity and resilience. Low HRV suggests accumulated stress, poor sleep, or overtraining.

Inflammation & Cellular
,
▲
Ferritin i
Optimal: 50 to 200 ng/mL
ng/mL
,
Your iron storage marker. High ferritin can indicate inflammation or iron overload, both worth investigating. Low ferritin means depleted iron stores, causing fatigue and poor exercise capacity, common and easily corrected.
Vitamin D (25-OH) i
Optimal: 100 to 150 nmol/L
nmol/L
,
A hormone more than a vitamin, involved in immune function, bone health, mood, and hundreds of genetic pathways. Low vitamin D is extremely common, especially in the UK, and linked to almost every chronic disease. High levels within range are protective and easily achieved through supplementation.
Homocysteine i
Optimal: <7 µmol/L
µmol/L
,
An amino acid byproduct that, at high levels, damages blood vessels and is linked to heart disease and cognitive decline. Low homocysteine is protective and usually reflects good B-vitamin status.
Omega-3 Index i
Optimal: >8%
%
,
The percentage of omega-3 fats in your red blood cell membranes, a strong marker of anti-inflammatory status. High omega-3 index is linked to better heart and brain health and even increased lifespan. Low levels are common in typical Western diets and easily corrected with fish oil.
