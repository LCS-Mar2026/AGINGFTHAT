Fibre: The Most Ignored Number in Healthspan After 40

Most people track protein. Almost nobody tracks fibre.

That is a problem, because fibre is one of the few nutritional levers with measurable effects on blood sugar, cholesterol, body composition and the gut microbiome.

It is also the nutrient most adults in the developed world are furthest from meeting.

THE NUMBER MOST PEOPLE MISS

Half the target, most of the time

National guidance for adults generally sits around 30 grams of fibre a day. Average intakes in the UK and similar countries sit closer to 15 to 20 grams.

That gap is not a minor dietary detail. It is a consistent, population-wide shortfall in one of the most researched components of the diet.

Fibre is not a single substance. It is a family of carbohydrates that resist digestion in the small intestine. Some types form gels. Some ferment in the colon. Some add bulk and speed transit. Each behaves differently, and the differences matter.

For anyone working on healthspan and biological age, fibre is attractive because it touches several systems at once.

WHAT FIBRE ACTUALLY DOES

A mechanical and a biological intervention

Fibre acts in two broad ways.

First, it changes the physical behaviour of the gut contents. Viscous soluble fibres thicken the mixture in the stomach and small intestine. That slows the release of glucose into the bloodstream and changes how bile acids and cholesterol are handled.

Second, it feeds bacteria in the colon. Those bacteria ferment fibre into short-chain fatty acids, principally butyrate, propionate and acetate. Those compounds are not waste. They feed the cells lining the colon, influence inflammation, and contribute to metabolic signalling.

So fibre is both a mechanical intervention and a biological one.

That second role is why fibre and gut diversity are usually discussed together. Different fibres feed different bacteria. A diet with one fibre source and a diet with twenty plant sources are not the same input, even at the same gram total.

FIBRE, BLOOD SUGAR AND CHOLESTEROL

The cholesterol effect is one of the clearest findings in nutrition research.

Soluble fibre binds bile acids in the gut. Bile acids are made from cholesterol. When they are carried out of the body, the liver uses circulating cholesterol to manufacture replacements. The net result is lower LDL cholesterol.

A meta-analysis by Whitehead and colleagues, published in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, examined soluble fibre and blood lipids and found consistent reductions in total and LDL cholesterol. Oat beta-glucan and psyllium have the strongest and most replicated evidence.

The glucose effect works through viscosity. A thicker gut contents slows the transfer of glucose across the intestinal wall, which flattens the post-meal rise. Studies of psyllium and beta-glucan have shown reduced post-meal glucose and improvements in longer-term glycaemic markers.

This is why fibre matters for anyone tracking blood markers of longevity. Fasting glucose, HbA1c, insulin and lipids all sit downstream of what happens in the gut after a meal.

FERMENTATION AND THE GUT LINING

The colon is not a passive tube. It is an ecosystem.

When fibre arrives undigested, bacteria ferment it. The short-chain fatty acids they produce are the primary fuel source for colon cells. Butyrate in particular is used directly by the cells lining the colon wall.

That is relevant beyond digestion. Short-chain fatty acid production is associated with the integrity of the gut barrier, with immune regulation, and with systemic inflammatory signalling. Inflammation sits close to the centre of most models of biological aging.

Research by O'Keefe and colleagues, published in Nature Microbiology, compared gut microbiome responses to different diets and found that fibre fermentation and short-chain fatty acid production responded to diet composition within a short period. The gut ecosystem is not fixed. It reacts to what arrives.

Two practical points follow.

Fibre variety matters alongside fibre quantity. Different plant fibres support different bacteria.

And the fermentation response takes time. A gut that has been fibre-poor for years does not adapt in a week.

BUILDING THE NUMBER WITHOUT SUFFERING

Most people who try to fix their fibre intake make the same mistake. They add a large amount of one high-fibre food all at once. The gut responds with bloating, gas and discomfort, and the experiment ends.

The better approach is gradual and structural.

1. Start with legumes. Lentils, chickpeas and beans are the highest-fibre foods per serving. They also deliver protein. A portion most days does more than any supplement.

2. Anchor breakfast. Oats, berries, nuts and seeds can put 10 grams into the first meal without effort.

3. Keep the skins on. Peeling and juicing remove fibre. Whole fruit beats juice on every measure that matters.

4. Count plants, not just grams. Aiming for 30 different plants a week builds fibre and microbiome diversity at the same time.

5. Increase slowly. Add a few grams per week. Keep fluid intake up. Give the microbiome time to adjust.

6. Use supplements as a fallback. Psyllium has the best evidence for cholesterol and glucose. It is a useful top-up, not a replacement for food.

WHY IT BELONGS IN A HEALTHSPAN PROGRAMME

Fibre is unglamorous, which is probably why it gets ignored.

But look at what sits downstream of it: fasting glucose, HbA1c, insulin, LDL cholesterol, inflammatory markers, gut diversity and body composition. Those are the same things a multivariable biological age assessment examines.

That is why fibre is one of the fastest places to intervene in a healthspan programme. It is cheap, it is measurable, and its effects show up in blood markers that can be retested.

One habit, several systems

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

How much fibre should someone over 40 eat?

Most national guidance sits around 30 grams a day for adults, and many people eat roughly half of that. Fibre needs increase gradually rather than overnight. Build up over several weeks, drink enough fluid, and spread fibre across meals so the gut can adapt without discomfort.

Does fibre lower cholesterol?

Soluble fibre binds bile acids in the gut, and the body then uses cholesterol to replace them. Beta-glucan from oats and barley, and psyllium, have the clearest cholesterol-lowering evidence. A meta-analysis by Whitehead and colleagues found consistent reductions in LDL cholesterol from soluble fibre intake.

Can fibre improve blood sugar control?

Viscous soluble fibre slows the rate at which glucose enters the bloodstream after a meal. Studies of psyllium and beta-glucan have shown reduced post-meal glucose and improved glycaemic markers. Whole-food fibre sources also increase satiety, which supports body composition and metabolic health.

Is a high-fibre diet bad for the gut?

A sudden large increase can cause bloating and gas while the microbiome adjusts. Increase gradually, keep fluid intake up, and include a range of fibre types from vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts and seeds. Persistent symptoms deserve a conversation with a qualified professional.

Which foods give the most fibre per serving?

Legumes lead, with lentils, chickpeas and beans providing substantial fibre per cooked cup. Whole grains, oats, barley, berries, pears, avocado, nuts and seeds all contribute. Aiming for 30 different plants a week builds fibre and microbiome diversity together.
