Healthspan depends less on salt alone than on the sodium-to-potassium ratio in the diet.

The Sodium-to-Potassium Ratio: The Number That Decides What Salt Actually Does to You

If you remember one number from this article, make it this one: around 1,600 mg of sodium to 4,700 mg of potassium per day. That is roughly the intake pattern the evidence points to, and it is the near-opposite of what most people over 40 actually eat.

Salt is not the villain it is usually made out to be. Sodium is essential. The problem is the ratio. Our ancestors ate diets where potassium dwarfed sodium by many times; the modern western diet inverts that relationship completely. In a 2005 analysis of ancestral diets, Cordain and colleagues estimated the sodium-to-potassium ratio of pre-agricultural diets at roughly 1:10 or better. The typical modern western diet runs at close to 1:0.5, more sodium than potassium (Cordain et al., American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 2005).

WHY THE RATIO BEATS SALT ALONE

Blood pressure research has quietly moved from "salt bad" to "the balance matters". The PURE study followed more than 100,000 adults across 18 countries and found that very high sodium intake raised cardiovascular risk, but so did very low intake: the relationship was J-shaped, with the lowest risk in the middle. Potassium, meanwhile, showed a simple, linear benefit: more potassium, lower blood pressure, fewer events (Mente et al., The Lancet, 2018).

A BMJ meta-analysis of randomised trials and cohort studies reached the same conclusion from the other direction: potassium supplementation lowered blood pressure, and the effect was strongest in people with the highest sodium intakes (Aburto et al., BMJ, 2013).

Put together, the picture is consistent: the ratio predicts cardiovascular risk better than either mineral on its own.

THE THREE FIXES

#1 Do not chase zero salt. Cutting sodium to the bone is not the win it looks like. The PURE data put the low-risk zone at moderate intake. Salting food to taste is compatible with the evidence; packaged and processed food is where the excess hides.

#2 Chase potassium instead. Beans, lentils, potatoes, avocados, bananas, leafy greens and yoghurt are all strong sources. Most adults take in roughly half the recommended potassium. If you are on blood pressure medication or have kidney disease, talk to your GP before pushing potassium hard.

#3 Eat like the ratio matters, because it does. A plate built around whole foods, with plants doing most of the work, moves the ratio in the right direction without a single gram of counting.
